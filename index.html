01What kind of camera do I have?

Two names are usually stamped somewhere on the body they're doing different jobs, like "Toyota" and "Corolla."

One name is the maker, the other is the model.

You'll likely also spot a shutter brand name, separate from both of those — the small mechanism that actually opens and closes to let light in was often bought in from a specialist company, not built in-house. Think of it as the "Intel Inside" of a hundred-year-old camera.

Is this guide for you?This one's written specifically around the Zeiss Ikon Nettar, a folding medium-format camera the exact rings, distances, and quirks described here come from that family of camera. If yours is a Zeiss Ikon Nettar, an Ikonta, or a similar folding cousin, most of this should map closely.

A nugget of historyZeiss started in 1846 as a small lens workshop in Germany. By the 1890s it was also building military optics — rangefinders, binoculars, submarine periscopes, rifle scopes — work that ramped up heavily through both World Wars. Today Zeiss still exists, still German, still making some of the world's most respected optics — including the precision lenses inside Apple's Vision Pro headset used for spatial computing. A hundred years separate your camera's lens from that one, made by the same company name.

02How old is it?

Somewhere near the lens, there's usually a stamped string of numbers that is similr to a birth certificate — assigned in order, the same way hospitals once numbered births on a ward.

Collectors have spent decades cross-referencing these numbers against old catalogs and dated cameras, building a rough family tree. Search the number alongside "shutter serial number chart" and you'll usually land within a year or two of the truth — not exact, but close enough.

For example Mine reads 1299979. Cross-referencing against a shutter dating chart, 1930 lands around 1,150,000 and 1931 lands around 1,500,000 — so mine falls right in that gap, putting the shutter's birth year at roughly 1930.

03What doesn't it have?

The fastest way to understand this camera is understand what modern cameras quietly do for you that this one flatly refuses to (and then appreciate why that is awesome).

No batteries, no charging

Fully mechanical, powered by springs and your own hands. It's also why it still works a century later — there's nothing inside to run out.

No auto-anything

No autofocus, no auto-exposure, no auto shutter reset. Every decision is yours, every single time, on purpose.

No screen to preview your shot

You won't know what you got until the roll comes back. Part of the learning curve is realizing the little viewfinder window doesn't line up perfectly with what the lens actually sees, especially up close.

No self-cocking mechanism

Like the hammer on an old gun. Nothing fires until you manually pull it back into position, every single time.

No stabilization

Your own stillness is the only stabilizer. Feet planted, elbows in, hold your breath a second before triggering the shutter.

04How do I open and close this safely?

Somewhere on the body there's a small button or catch — press it, and the whole front of the camera pops open on its own, springing outward like a pop-up book.

Don't stop the moment it looks open, though. Keep going until you feel or hear it settle firmly — the little rails it rides on should lock rigid and straight, not wobble. A wobbly, half-open front means the lens isn't sitting the correct distance from the film, which throws everything else off before you've even started.

How to tell it's really lockedGive the extended front a gentle nudge with one finger. Locked, it won't budge. Not locked, it'll wiggle or want to fold back in on you — a clear, physical answer with no guessing involved.

Closing it back up takes gentle pressure on the locked hinges to release them, then gently guide the front back in flat.

05Meet the moving parts

Let's had some fun moving the many mechanical parts while learning their purpose and name.

Photo goes here
The camera fully open, with the parts named on this page pointed out or labeled
the unfold

a.k.a. the bed release

The button or catch that pops the whole front of the camera open, springing the lens forward on its rails until it locks into shooting position — like releasing the catch on a pop-up book.

the accordion

a.k.a. the bellows

The pleated material connecting lens to body — folds flat for the case, just like the instrument, and extends out into playing position when you're ready to shoot. Not airtight just a light-blocking flexible tunnel.

the window

a.k.a. the viewfinder

The small window you actually look through to compose a shot. It's a separate peephole from the lens itself, with no connection to focus at all — what you see through it never changes, no matter where the focus ring is set.

Photo goes here
A visual showing parallax — the viewfinder and the lens seeing slightly different frames up close
the porthole

a.k.a. the red window

The small round window on the back, often tinted red or amber. This camera doesn't count frames automatically — the film's paper backing has numbers printed on it, and this window is a peephole for reading them as you wind, like checking an odometer for mileage through a tiny opening. The tint isn't decoration either: it filters the light passing through so it doesn't fog the film sitting right behind it. Worth keeping its little sliding metal cover closed except for the moment you're winding the film.

the hammer

a.k.a. the cocking lever

The part you pull back before every shot, arming the spring. Skip this and pressing the shutter does nothing at all, the button will go down but the shutter will not open/close.

the trigger

a.k.a. the shutter button

The button on top you actually press to fire. It doesn't do the firing itself — it sends your press down through a small internal pin to trip the real mechanism near the lens.

the guess

a.k.a. the focus ring

The small center ring. No live preview confirms you're right — you estimate distance and trust a printed scale. Less "focusing," more "aiming."

the eye

a.k.a. the aperture ring

Works like a pupil. Wide open in dim light, squeezed small in bright light.

the blink

a.k.a. the shutter speed ring

Controls how long the eye stays open. A fast blink freezes motion. A slow blink lets in more light but shows every wobble in your hands.

06How can I confirm the shutter is working right?

The quirk of learning about an old mechanical camera is not knowing what "working correctly" is even supposed to feel like. So let's learn that first, with no film loaded, no pressure. You might determine your camera needs some repair, cleaning you are good to purchase your first role of film.

Pull back the trigger. Press the courier. Watch closely: it should fire with a quick, clean blink, and the button should pop back up on its own, ready to go again. Here is what it sounds like on my camera.

Alignment is everythingIf the button sticks down instead of popping back up, check that the accordion is pulled all the way out and locked rigid first — a loose, not-fully-extended accordion can throw off the release mechanism's alignment entirely. The shutter button lever can align above undstand of below the shutter trigger. You can manually reset the shutter level.

A few more gentle, no-cost checks while you're getting acquainted:

  • Check the accordion for pinholes. In a dark room, shine a phone flashlight inside it and look for tiny escaping points of light.
  • Turn every ring, gently. Stiff is normal for its age. Frozen solid is not.
  • Try a firm, quick press instead of a slow one. Old lubricant turns tacky, not smooth — a confident tap often succeeds where a gentle push stalls, the way a stuck drawer needs one good tug rather than a steady pull.
Say this"Can you give it a CLA?" — clean, lubricate, adjust. It's the standard service name for exactly this kind of tune-up. Think of it as a spa day for the shutter.

Once cocking and firing feels familiar in your hands — once you know what "right" feels like without having to think about it — you're genuinely ready.

07What do all the tiny numbers means?

Here are the numbers you will find on this camera:

f-stops4.5 · 5.6 · 8 · 11 · 16 · 22 · 32
shutter speedsB · 1 · 2 · 5 · 10 · 25 · 50 · 100 · 250
focus, in meters1 · 1.5 · 1.7 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5 · 8 · 15 · ∞

Your exact camera may vary slightly.

What do you notice?Look closely at the focus distances: 1, 1.5, 1.7, 2 — all bunched tightly together — then 5, 8, 15, ∞, spreading further and further apart. Before reading on, take a guess: why would a lens maker space them out like that instead of evenly?

Here's the answer: tiny shifts in the glass matter enormously for something close, and barely matter at all for something far. A hair's width of movement changes focus dramatically at arm's length, but that same hair's width barely nudges anything once you're focusing on something down the block. The spacing on the ring is really a map of how much the glass has to physically move — not a map of even distance.

What does "B" stand for?Bulb. Before mechanical timers, photographers fired the shutter by squeezing a small rubber air bulb by hand, holding it as long as they wanted the shutter open. The rubber bulbs disappeared generations ago — the letter stuck around anyway.
What does the ∞ symbol actually mean here?Not "infinitely far away" in the literal sense — more like "far enough that it stops mattering." Past a certain distance, a tree fifty feet away and a mountain five miles away land in focus identically. The lens simply stops trying to tell far things apart from farther things.

08How do I know which way to turn the focus?

Forget clockwise and counterclockwise — every camera engraves its rings a little differently, so that memory trick betrays you eventually. Here's one that never lies: watch the glass, not the numbers.

On the focus ring, the small center lens physically creeps forward, toward your subject, as you focus closer. It sinks back, toward infinity, the more it retreats. You don't need to remember a direction — just watch or feel whether the glass is coming toward your subject, or away.

Worth doing onceTurn each ring all the way to both its hard stops, just to feel where the range begins and ends. After that, your hands will remember far better than your eyes ever will.
Listen, tooTurn the guess slowly and you can actually hear it working — a faint threading sound as the front glass travels forward and back on a fine internal screw. Only that front piece moves; the other two elements sit fixed further back inside the housing. It's a small thing, but it's a nice confirmation you're feeling and hearing a real, precise mechanism doing exactly what it was built to do.

09How close can I get to my subject?

Every lens like this has a limit of distance it can't see past — a closest point it can still bring into sharp focus. Step closer than that, and no amount of careful guessing on the focus ring will save the shot. It simply softens and blurrs.

Here's a plain-language gut check: if you could comfortably reach out and shake your subject's hand without leaning in, you're likely too close. Think dinner-table distance, not doorway-greeting distance — roughly the space between two people having a relaxed conversation, not a hug.

Find your camera's real numberTurn the focus ring to its tightest, closest-feeling end — the one furthest from the infinity mark. Whatever's printed there is your camera's actual hard limit. Worth checking once and remembering, since it's different from a phone or modern camera that happily lets you get inches away.
10 — What kind of film do I ask for at the camera store (or online store)?

This is the part all the reading was for. Walk in with your camera, not just a question — let them hold it, let them see what you've been getting to know. No shop nearby? The same question works typed into a search bar.

Say this"I have a vintage medium format foldable camera and need some help selecting and loading film for my first shots."
That one sentence does a lot of work: it doesn't assume you know the jargon, it tells them exactly what kind of camera you're working with, and it signals you want help, not just a shelf pointed out.

This isn't the same film as a disposable camera. It's a wider, older roll format — a bigger canvas, wrapped in paper instead of sealed in plastic — and a good shop will have opinions about the best starter stock for a camera like yours.

Let them choose with you

Film shop staff tend to be enthusiasts, not just clerks. Most are genuinely glad to help someone starting out, and can steer you toward something forgiving for a first roll.

Let them show you the rest

Loading the roll, threading the paper leader, advancing to the first frame — ask them to walk you through it in person. Hands-on, once, from someone who's done it a thousand times, beats any written instructions.

What happens next — the actual loading, the actual shooting, the actual roll — that part's yours to discover in the room, with people who love this as much as you're about to.

A hundred years of small decisions

Every photograph this camera has ever made required a person to choose, by hand, what distance between the subject and the camera, how much light to let in and how to stabalize the shot with their breath. That hasn't changed. You're not learning how to simply operate a mechanical camera you're learning how to have a conversation with one.

I inherited this camera without knowing how to use it, so I learned it in real time — asking questions of my photography buff friends, testing settings, taking it apart with my eyes if not my hands.
